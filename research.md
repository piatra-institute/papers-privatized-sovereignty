## The 2026 exhibit set (T1, primary filings, all fetched from EDGAR this session)

- **Space Exploration Technologies Corp. (2026)**, Prospectus (Form 424B4), June 12, 2026, and Form 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 2026. IPO priced at $135.00 per share, listed on Nasdaq as SPCX. At completion Musk held approximately **82.4 percent of the voting power** (82.3 with the greenshoe) through 849,494,440 Class A and 5,219,053,075 Class B shares, approximately **91.6 percent of the Class B**. Class A carries one vote, Class B ten, Class C none. Class B, voting separately, elects **51 percent of the authorized directors**. Controlled-company exemptions invoked under Nasdaq rules. From the 10-Q cover (July 28, 2026): 7,696,293,669 Class A and 5,485,486,276 Class B outstanding, so Musk's stake is roughly **46 percent of shares** against 82.4 percent of votes.
- **Snap Inc. (2026)**, Form 10-K for fiscal 2025, filed February 5, 2026. The two co-founders "control over **99 percent** of the voting power"; the publicly traded Class A carries **no vote**; each founder holds a proxy over the other's shares effective on death or disability, so the survivor "could individually control nearly all of the voting power"; a 2016 dividend of non-voting shares exists to let the founders sell economic interest without losing control.
- **Meta Platforms, Inc. (2026)**, DEF 14A, April 2026. Zuckerberg: **60.8 percent** of total voting power via 341,823,978 Class B shares, **99.8 percent** of the class; controlled company under Nasdaq rules; the board de-classifies only if Class B loses its majority.
- **Alphabet Inc. (2026)**, DEF 14A, April 2026. Page **27.4** and Brin **25.3 percent** of voting power (46.5 and 42.9 percent of Class B respectively), jointly **52.7 percent**; Class B ten votes, Class C zero.
- **Palantir Technologies Inc. (2026)**, DEF 14A, April 2026. The Class F mechanism verbatim: for the covered proposals "the voting power of the Class F common stock shall equal **49.999999%** of the voting power of all of the outstanding shares ... less the voting power of the shares of Class A common stock and Class B common stock subject to the Founder Voting Agreement"; 1,005,000 Class F shares in a founder voting trust (Cohen, Karp, Thiel) against 2,295,915,766 Class A.
- **Ford Motor Company (2026)**, DEF 14A, March 2026. Class B fixed at **40 percent** of general voting power; on the record date 3,940,230,185 common against 70,852,076 Class B, so the family class is roughly **1.8 percent of shares** carrying 40 percent of votes; the company's own opposition statement to the equal-vote proposal describes the structure as a 70-year term of admission since the 1956 listing.

## The dual-class lifecycle (T1)

- **Cremers, Lauterbach and Pajuste (2024)**, RCFS 13: 459–493 (10.1093/rcfs/cfac026): dual-class firms show a valuation premium at IPO that dissipates and turns to discount roughly six to nine years out.
- **Council of Institutional Investors (2018)**, petitions to NYSE and Nasdaq, October 24, 2018: asked both exchanges to require conversion to one-share-one-vote within seven years of IPO; summarizes the premium-fades-to-discount evidence.
- **Bebchuk and Kastiel (2017)**, Virginia Law Review 103: 585–630: the case for perpetual dual-class is untenable on its own lifecycle logic; if control is justified by one founder's ability it cannot survive the founder's departure, decay, or heirs.
- **Gompers, Ishii and Metrick (2010)**, RFS 23: 1051–1088 (10.1093/rfs/hhp024): firm value rises with insiders' cash-flow rights and falls with insider voting rights; the wedge itself is the harm variable.
- **Masulis, Wang and Xie (2009)**, JF 64: 1697–1727 (10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01477.x): as the wedge widens, cash holdings are worth less to outside shareholders, CEO pay is higher, value-destroying acquisitions are likelier, and capital expenditure contributes less to value: the price of control without exposure, measured.
- **Dyck and Zingales (2004)**, JF 59: 537–600 (10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00642.x): private benefits of control across 39 countries, block-premium method; the measured B that finances entrenchment.
- **Grossman and Hart (1988)**, JFE 20: 175–202: one-share-one-vote aligns votes with value precisely because it forces a would-be controller to hold cash-flow exposure; deviations create private-benefit contests.
- **Jensen and Meckling (1976)**, JFE 3: 305–360: agency costs and the nexus-of-contracts frame; the intellectual instrument the contractual turn used.

## The exchange history and the race (T1)

- **Seligman (1986)**, George Washington Law Review 54: 687: the NYSE refused non-voting common from 1926, formalized one-share-one-vote in 1940, and moved to abandon it in 1986 under competition from Amex and Nasdaq; the classic documented holdout-and-collapse.
- **Business Roundtable v. SEC**, 905 F.2d 406 (D.C. Cir. 1990): Rule 19c-4, the SEC's 1988 floor against voting-rights reductions, vacated as beyond the Commission's authority; the removed federal floor.
- **Financial Conduct Authority (2024)**, PS24/6, UK Listing Rules effective July 29, 2024: the single commercial-companies category permits weighted voting held by natural persons **indefinitely, with no sunset and no ratio cap**; institutional pre-IPO holders capped at a ten-year sunset.
- **Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing (2026)**, Consultation Conclusions, Listing Framework Competitiveness Review, effective July 24, 2026: weighted-voting market-cap thresholds halved (Test A HK$40bn to HK$20bn; Test B HK$10bn to HK$6bn with revenue HK$1bn to HK$600m), and issuers at or above HK$40bn may adopt ratios up to **20:1** (from the previous 10:1 cap).
- **Congressional Research Service (2021)**, "Dual Class Stock: Background and Policy Debate," In Focus IF11992: the policy state of play; notes sunset heterogeneity.

## Passive ownership (T1)

- **Bebchuk and Hirst (2022)**, "Big Three Power, and Why It Matters," Boston University Law Review 102: 1547: at end-2021 the Big Three held a median stake of **21.9 percent** in S&P 500 companies, about **24.9 percent** of votes cast at their meetings.
- **Broadridge (2025)**, ProxyPulse 2025: retail investors voted **28 percent** of the shares they owned in the 2025 proxy season, the lowest in nine years.
- **Hirschman (1970)**, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (Harvard UP): the vocabulary for what indexing forecloses: exit is automatic (an index cannot leave), voice is delegated, loyalty is unasked.

## The state's condition (T1/T2)

- **Pew Research Center (2025)**, "Public Trust in Government: 1958–2025," December 4, 2025: **17 percent** trust the federal government to do what is right always or most of the time (2 plus 15), against **73 percent** when first asked in 1958.
- **Light (2019)**, *The Government-Industrial Complex* (Oxford UP): the federal workforce of contract and grant employees vastly outnumbers civil servants; policymaking capacity retained, delivery capacity outsourced.
- **Liscow, Nober and Slattery (2023)**, NBER WP 31705: experienced in-house public engineers materially reduce infrastructure project costs; capacity treated as overhead is expensive.
- **Tabakovic and Wollmann (2018)**, NBER WP 24638: patent examiners grant significantly more, and lower-quality, patents to firms that later hire them; capture without bribery, through the labor market.
- **Rahman (2018)**, Cardozo Law Review 39: 1621–1692: the public-utility concept revived for platforms controlling social infrastructure.

## Asia, five systems (T1)

- **Singapore**: Constitution of the Republic of Singapore, **Fifth Schedule**: Temasek is a listed entity whose board and chief executive appointments and removals require presidential concurrence, tied to the protection of past reserves. **Ang and Ding (2006)**, JMFM 16: 64–88 (10.1016/j.mulfin.2005.04.010): Singapore government-linked companies show higher valuations and stronger governance scores than matched non-GLCs; the custodial model has measurable governance content.
- **China**: revised **Company Law of the PRC** (adopted December 29, 2023, effective July 1, 2024): codified governance overhaul, capital rules, audit committees, controlling-shareholder liability, inside a party-sovereign order. **Brødsgaard (2012)**, China Quarterly 211: 624–648: party organization and the nomenklatura system inside the largest business groups; the party above the charter.
- **Japan**: **Franks, Mayer and Miyajima (2014)**, RFS 27: 2580–2625 (10.1093/rfs/hhu018): twentieth-century Japanese ownership: dispersed between the wars, then insider/cross-shareholding stabilization. **Tokyo Stock Exchange (2023)**, request of March 31, 2023, to Prime and Standard companies for "management that is conscious of cost of capital and stock price"; over 40 percent of listed companies then traded below book. **Japan Exchange Group (2026)**, update of April 28, 2026: the request revised with emphasis on capital allocation; disclosure-status lists published continuously.
- **Korea**: **Bae, Kang and Kim (2002)**, JF 57: 2695–2740 (10.1111/1540-6261.00510): chaebol mergers benefit controlling families at minority expense; tunneling measured. **Republic of Korea (2025)**: Commercial Act amendments: Act No. 20991 (promulgated July 22, 2025; fiduciary duty extended to shareholders; 3 percent cap effective July 22, 2026) and the second 2025 amendment (cumulative voting mandatory for large listed companies from September 2025 promulgation; at least two audit-committee directors separately elected, effective around September 2026).
- **India**: **Bertrand, Mehta and Mullainathan (2002)**, QJE 117: 121–148 (10.1162/003355302753399463): tunneling through Indian business-group pyramids, measured via earnings-shock propagation. **OECD (2014)**, *Improving Corporate Governance in India* (10.1787/9789264220652-en): related-party transactions and minority protection under promoter dominance. **National Stock Exchange of India (2025)**, India Ownership Tracker, September 2025: promoter ownership steady at about **50.1 percent** of NSE-listed value.
- **Cross-cutting**: **La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer (1999)**, JF 54: 471–517: concentrated control, not dispersion, is the world norm. **Claessens, Djankov and Lang (2000)**, JFE 58: 81–112: the separation of ownership and control across nine East Asian economies; pyramids and crossholdings as wedge machinery.

## Decline canon (T1, framing)

- **Berle and Means (1932)**, *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* (Macmillan): the first stratum's constitution.
- **Olson (1982)**, *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (Yale UP): distributional coalitions accumulate until they rigidify the order.
- **Tainter (1988)**, *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (Cambridge UP): declining marginal returns on complexity; collapse as simplification, often invisible until fast.
- **North, Wallis and Weingast (2009)**, *Violence and Social Orders* (CUP): open-access versus limited-access orders; personalized control of organizations is the limited-access signature.
