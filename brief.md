# Brief

Written before research begins.

## Question

A corporation can be public in its financing, public in its systemic consequences, public in its subsidies, public in its pension exposure, and public in its national importance, while remaining private in its internal constitution: one person holds a class of shares that outvotes everyone who paid. The state that would treat a discrete violent act as an existential threat has no category for this transformation, because every step of it was disclosed, priced, and individually lawful. How did control without economic exposure become the normal constitution of the most consequential firms, what does that normalization say about the order that permits it, and what distinguishes hierarchies that can still correct themselves from hierarchies that cannot?

## Claim

The founder-controlled public corporation is a fossil of a larger condition: the loss of institutional capacity for correction. Five moves carry the paper:

1. **The wedge as diagnostic object.** The quantity that matters is the separation between voting control and cash-flow ownership. The wedge severs four things classical ownership joined: who receives the losses, who chooses the board, who directs the enterprise, and who bears the external consequences. The 2026 exhibit set is verified from primary filings: a founder taking a company public in June 2026 while keeping 82.4 percent of the vote; a company whose publicly traded class carries no votes at all; a charter whose voting formula contains the constant 49.999999 percent; a family class holding 40 percent of the vote on under 2 percent of the shares.
2. **Self-financing entrenchment.** A controller with economic stake alpha internalizes alpha of the value his control destroys and all of the private benefits control yields, so the smaller the stake, the cheaper it is to keep control that has become bad. The wedge does not merely permit entrenchment; it pays for it. This inequality is the paper's sharpest formal point and the simulation prices it.
3. **Stratigraphy of normalization.** The arrangement was not chosen; it was deposited in six layers: the managerial republic and its exchange-enforced voting norm; the contractual turn that converted a constitutional question into a disclosure question; the founder counterrevolution funded by abundant venture capital and survivor mythology; platform sovereignty that gave firms state-like functions and states firm-dependence; passive ownership that stretched shareholder consent into metaphysics; and a geopolitical market for corporate constitutions in which exchanges compete by relaxing them.
4. **Legible versus denied hierarchy.** The Asian comparison shows hierarchy is not the pathology. Singapore, China, Japan, Korea, and India are all more explicitly hierarchical than the United States; they differ on five dimensions: visibility of the controller, economic reciprocity, mandate, removability, and informational correction. The distinctive American form is hierarchy denied: personalized sovereignty described as market outcome. The dangerous configuration on any continent is concentrated authority protected from correction.
5. **Endogenous defeat.** An institution formidable against frontal attack is most cheaply degraded through its selection mechanisms: loyalty replaces competence, truthful reporting becomes career-limiting, maintenance loses to monuments, and inherited capability conceals declining decision quality until a crisis demands actual performance. The fortress is not stormed; it stops being able to distinguish a steward from an owner.

The closing sections translate the diagnosis into observable correction events with falsifiers (sunset conversions, succession, the Japanese unwind, the Korean reforms) and a constitutional settlement: time- and event-bounded superior votes, renewal by the unaffiliated, restricted domains, a public control statement, obligations for sovereign-function firms, and rebuilt public technical capacity. The paper states what would falsify it.

## Kind

**genealogy + formal-model**, ships a simulation. `has_simulation: true`, `claims_target: results.json`.

Three mechanisms, each with calibration anchors and invariants:

- **The lifecycle of the wedge.** Founder-specific knowledge decays while private benefits of control persist; insulation from the market is worth most exactly when the firm is young. Calibrated so the insulation premium crosses zero inside the documented six-to-nine-year window. Charter regimes compared: perpetual wedge, hard time sunset, event sunset, renewable sunset by unaffiliated vote, single class. Must produce: the early premium is real; the perpetual regime destroys value after the crossing; the renewable sunset captures the early premium without the late discount; and the self-financing entrenchment threshold, the economic stake below which a rational controller keeps value-destroying control, priced against the verified 2026 wedge exhibits.
- **The race in listing standards.** Exchanges compete for scarce founders; the revenue from a listing is private to the exchange and the governance cost is diffuse. Must reproduce the documented shape: a restrictive incumbent standard stable for decades while founder demand for insulation is low, cascading in a few years once demand rises and one venue defects; a coordinated floor holds at any demand level; and the floor's removal is followed by completion of the cascade. Anchors: the New York Stock Exchange's fifty-year voting norm and its 1986 abandonment under competition; the 1988 federal floor voided in 1990; London and Hong Kong in 2024 and 2026.
- **Correction capacity under five architectures.** One organization, one controller whose quality can fail, one stock of inherited capability that conceals the failure while it lasts. Architectures differ on reporting fidelity, removability, and reciprocity: sealed wedge, reciprocal majority owner, state-custodial, party-vertical, network-relational. Must produce: output-based detection is structurally late everywhere because the stock buffers the flow; the sealed wedge maximizes damage because detection does not trigger removal; the party-vertical architecture removes fast but detects late when loyalty selection corrupts reporting; the network architecture rarely removes but also dampens extremes; and the honest result that the sealed wedge under a good controller is the best performer in normal times, which is precisely why the tail is invisible until it arrives.

Anchored magnitudes enter results.json as cited records: the verified 2026 voting and ownership figures, the six-to-nine-year premium-to-discount window, the turnover-insensitivity finding for dual-class firms.

## Constraint

The paper stands alone and cites no PIATRA paper. Moral gravity is handled once and cleanly: the comparison that motivated the seed is reformulated as a statement about what the state can perceive, never as an equivalence of harms; no living person is accused of anything beyond what filings disclose. The correction-events section is analytical, states falsifiers, and gives no personalized advice, no tickers to buy, and no portfolio. Asia is five systems, not one; none is romanticized, and China's correction pathology is stated with the same directness as America's. Every voting figure, date, and statute is verified against the primary record before use; the seed's unverifiable market color (an August 2026 index drawdown, a named conglomerate's unwind size) is dropped. Institutional authors are spelled out in text for the refs matcher.

## Cornerstone literature

- **Berle and Means** — the separation of ownership and control, first stratum.
- **Jensen and Meckling** — the nexus-of-contracts reconception; also the origin of the agency frame the wedge breaks.
- **Grossman and Hart** — one-share-one-vote theory; when deviations are efficient and when they shield control.
- **Seligman** — the documented history of the exchange voting norm, 1926 to 1986.
- **Business Roundtable v. SEC** — the voided federal floor.
- **Bebchuk and Kastiel; Cremers, Lauterbach and Pajuste; Gompers, Ishii and Metrick; Masulis, Wang and Xie** — the dual-class lifecycle: IPO premium, decay window, agency costs, turnover insensitivity.
- **Dyck and Zingales** — private benefits of control, measured; the B in the entrenchment inequality.
- **La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer; Claessens, Djankov and Lang** — concentrated control worldwide; the wedge in Asia.
- **Bebchuk and Hirst; Broadridge** — the Big Three and the vanishing citizen-shareholder.
- **Hirschman** — exit, voice, and loyalty; what passive ownership forecloses.
- **Olson; Tainter; North, Wallis and Weingast** — decline as distributional sclerosis, complexity cost, and limited-access order.
- **Rahman** — platform power as utility power.
- **Ang and Ding; the Fifth Schedule** — Singapore's custodial architecture.

- **Tabakovic and Wollmann; Liscow, Nober and Slattery; Light; Pew** — the state's informational and capability decline.
